Finite-noise corrections to Kramers escape in the symmetric quartic double well

A. Author1 and B. Coauthor2

2Institute for Statistical Mechanics, Sample Institute, Sample Town 00000, Country

(Received 17 November 2026)

Kramers’ formula for the rate of thermally activated escape is exact only when the barrier is high compared with the noise strength. For the symmetric quartic double well we compute the escape rate of an overdamped particle exactly, from the mean first-passage time, and compare it with Kramers’ formula for barrier heights from ΔU/D = 0.4 to 6. The formula overestimates the rate by up to 14% for noise strengths D below 0.22 and underestimates it by 31% at D = 0.5; to leading order in D the relative correction is numerically −3D/2. The quadrature is checked against Langevin simulations.

Introduction.—Thermally activated escape over a barrier is the elementary process behind reaction rates, nucleation, and the switching of bistable devices [1–4]. For a particle that moves in a potential U(x) with strong friction and is driven by white noise of strength D, the rate of escape from a well was obtained by Kramers [2]. His result is an asymptotic one: it holds when the barrier height ΔU is large compared with D, and the reviews [3,5] discuss the conditions under which it can be trusted. How good it is at moderate ΔU/D, where experiments on small systems usually operate, is a quantitative question that is easiest to answer in a case where the exact rate is known.

Model.—We take the overdamped Langevin equation

dxdt=−U′(x)+2Dξ(t)(1)

with ⟨ξ(t)ξ(t′)⟩=δ(t−t′), in the symmetric double well

U(x)=x44−x22(2)

The minima are at xa = ±1, where U″ = 2, the top of the barrier is at xb = 0, where U″ = −1, and the barrier height is ΔU = 1/4 (Fig. 1).

Kramers’ formula.—For ΔU/D → ∞ the rate of escape from the well at xa over the barrier at xb is [2,3]

rK=U″(xa)|U″(xb)|2πe−ΔU/D(3)

which for the potential (2) is

rK=22πe−1/(4D)(4)

It is Eq. (4) that we compare with the exact rate.

Exact rate.—In one variable the mean first-passage time can be written in closed form [4,5]. A particle that starts at x0 in the left well, and is absorbed at the top of the barrier, needs the time

τ(x0)=1D∫x00dyeU(y)/D∫−∞ydze−U(z)/D(5)

when the left side is reflecting. At the barrier top the particle is equally likely to fall to either side, so only every second arrival there ends in the other well, and the escape rate is

r=12τ(−1)(6)

We evaluate Eq. (5) by the trapezoid rule on grids of 6×105 and 2×104 points; halving the grids changes the rate by less than 2×10−11 (relative) for D = 0.05, 0.1 and 0.3.

As a check we integrated Eq. (1) directly by the Euler–Maruyama method, with a time step of 10−3 and 4000 paths for each of five noise strengths. The rates agree with the quadrature to within 6%; they are the lower ones, as expected of a method that can miss the crossing of the barrier top between two steps.

Results.—Table I and Fig. 2 give the rate, and Fig. 3 its ratio to Kramers’ value. The formula overestimates the exact rate for every D below 0.22, by at most 14% (near D = 0.09, ΔU/D = 2.7). At D ≈ 0.22 the two agree, and for larger D the formula underestimates the rate, by 31% at D = 0.5. The agreement near D = 0.22 is only where the deviation changes its sign: at ΔU/D = 1.2 the barrier is hardly one, and the agreement says nothing about the validity of the formula.

For D → 0 the ratio of the rates approaches one as

rrK=1−32D+O(D2)(7)

The coefficient is a numerical result: the quotient (r/rK − 1)/D, evaluated at D = 2.5, 5, and 7.5 × 10−3 and extrapolated linearly to D = 0, gives −1.50 (dotted line in Fig. 3). We have not derived it analytically; an asymptotic expansion in D, as in Refs. [3,4], would serve to confirm it.

Corrected formula.—Taken at face value, Eq. (7) suggests the corrected rate

rc=rK(1−32D)(8)

Table II compares it with Eq. (4). The corrected formula is within 1% of the exact rate up to D = 0.038 and within 5% up to D = 0.11; beyond that the linear term overshoots, because the ratio turns around at D ≈ 0.09 (Fig. 3) while Eq. (8) keeps falling. The correction is therefore useful in the range where Kramers’ formula is already good, and not beyond it.

Conclusion.—In the symmetric quartic double well Kramers’ formula is within 14% of the exact rate where it overestimates it, and the leading relative correction is −3D/2, which improves the formula only up to D = 0.11. The size of the deviation at the noise strengths where ΔU/D is a few should be kept in mind when rates are compared with Kramers’ formula, as it is in work on stochastic resonance [6,7].
