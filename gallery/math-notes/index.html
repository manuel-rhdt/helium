Gaussian integrals and their relatives

From e−x2 to Stirling’s formula

1The basic integral

The function f(x)=e−x2 has no elementary antiderivative, yet its integral over the whole line can be found exactly. The trick, due to Poisson, is to square the integral and pass to polar coordinates.

Theorem 1

The Gaussian integral has the value

∫−∞∞e−x2dx=π.(1)

Proof. Write I for the integral. Then I2 is a double integral over the plane, and in polar coordinates (r,θ) the factor r from the Jacobian makes the radial integral elementary:

I2=∫−∞∞∫−∞∞e−(x2+y2)dxdy=∫02π∫0∞e−r2rdrdθ=2π·12=π.(2)

Since the integrand is positive, I=π.

∎

6The Fourier transform

Finally, the Gaussian is its own Fourier transform, up to scale. With the convention below, the function g(x)=e−πx2 is a fixed point of the transform, which is why it minimises the product of the widths of a function and of its spectrum:

g^(ξ)=∫−∞∞g(x)e−2πixξdx=e−πξ2.(8)

The proof is a single application of (3) with a=π and b=−2πiξ: the exponent b2/4a equals −πξ2, and the prefactor π/a is 1.
